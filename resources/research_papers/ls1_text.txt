Predictive models for inventory 
optimization: a machine learning application 
for demand forecasting at a construction 
supplies distributor
Diocélio Dornela Goulart1,2*, Rodrigo Baroni de Carvalho2*, Mariana Almeida Henriques2 and 
Bárbara Kawany Gonçalves Nunes Carvalho2 
Abstract 
Inventory management is a critical task for distribution companies, as forecasting errors can lead to excess 
inventory, higher storage costs, or stockouts. This practice and policy paper presents a single-case study describing 
the development and deployment of a machine learning-based predictive model designed to optimize inventory 
management for a medium-sized Brazilian construction supplies distributor. Using the Amazon Forecast and Amazon 
SageMaker Canvas platforms, the study demonstrated how advanced demand forecasting techniques can potentially 
reduce stockouts and enhance operational efficiency. The research compared various predictive algorithms 
and assessed their performance using metrics such as RMSE, MAPE, MASE, and WAPE. The model achieved a weighted 
absolute percentage error (WAPE) of approximately 0.69% during the evaluation period, with performance varying 
across SKUs and demand patterns. The evaluation employed an 80/20 train-test split with a 60-day forecast horizon. 
Rather than claiming algorithmic novelty, this study’s primary contribution is architectural and socio-technical since it 
documents an end-to-end implementation blueprint for deploying cloud-based AutoML in an SME that previously 
relied solely on descriptive analytics. The reliance on a single aggregate metric, the absence of baseline comparisons, 
limited transparency into proprietary AutoML, and the lack of quantitative measurement of business impact 
are discussed in the limitations of the study. The study details the technology architecture, data governance 
workflow, and human–AI interaction protocols, providing technical and managerial recommendations for SMEs 
seeking to transition from retrospective reporting to simplified predictive analysis, even with limited IT resources 
and without specialized Data Science teams.

management (SCM)

Theoretical background
This section provides an overview of the theoretical 
framework that guided the application development. 
Inventory management is central to logistics and 
operations management, balancing inventory costs 
with the risk of product shortages. Accurate demand 
forecasting is essential for effective inventory control 
[9]. 
Traditional 
forecasting 
techniques, 
such 
as 
moving averages, exponential smoothing, and ARIMA 
(Autoregressive integrated moving average) models, are 
widely used but may have limitations when handling 
complex, seasonal, or multivariate data [5].
Demand in the retail and distribution industry, such as 
construction supplies, often exhibits characteristics like 
intermittency (periods without demand) and seasonality. 
Traditional models may struggle to capture these 
patterns accurately [20, 21]. Additionally, external factors 
such as promotions, economic conditions, changes in 
tax laws, political events, competitor activities, and even 
weather can influence demand, requiring models that can 
incorporate multiple sources of information.
Predictive models using Artificial Intelligence (AI) for 
inventory management offer significant advantages over 
traditional methods. ML algorithms process many factors 
and identify complex patterns that traditional techniques 
overlook [3], resulting in more accurate demand 
forecasts and enabling quick adaptation to changing 
market conditions [12]. As a subfield of AI, ML provides 
alternative and complementary methods to traditional 
statistical forecasting techniques. ML algorithms can 
identify complex patterns in historical data, including 
nonlinear relationships and interactions among multiple 
variables, without relying on strict statistical assumptions 
[15].
Product demand forecasting is an essential technique 
for inventory management, enabling companies to 
optimize stock levels, reduce costs, and enhance 
customer service. One machine learning method that 
has gained prominence in this field is random forest for 
forecasting [24]. Other machine learning algorithms have 
been used effectively for demand forecasting, including 
artificial neural networks (ANNs), support vector 
machines (SVMs), and decision tree-based methods such 
as random forest and gradient boosting [22, 24]. These 
approaches are especially valuable when large amounts of

data (Big Data) are available and demand is driven by a 
complex mix of factors [6].
Multiple studies show that this technique (Random 
Forest) is highly effective for inventory management. Van 
Steenbergen and Mes [23] introduced DemandForest, an 
innovative approach that combines K-means clustering, 
random forests, and quantile regression forests to 
forecast demand patterns for new products. The method 
was tested on real datasets and outperformed several 
reference methods, resulting in about 15% savings in 
inventory [23]. Vairagade et  al. [24] compared various 
demand forecasting models, including random forest and 
artificial neural networks. They found that random forest 
was more accurate, making it better suited for predicting 
product demand in complex supply chains.
On the other hand, Tian et  al. [21] addressed 
intermittent demand forecasting, a common challenge 
in retail, and proposed a combined approach that 
incorporates current inventory and past sales. This 
method, known as the Markov-combined method 
(MCM), 
proved 
more 
accurate 
than 
traditional 
techniques, leading to significant improvements in 
inventory management.
Punia et  al. [17] introduced another combined 
approach that uses long short-term memory (LSTM) 
and random forest models to capture complex temporal 
and regression relationships. This method significantly 
outperformed other forecasting techniques in accuracy 
and robustness in multichannel retail settings.
Naik et al. [16] examined sales forecasting in the food 
industry using random forest regression. This model 
was notable for its high accuracy compared with other 
machine learning methods, making it well suited for 
predicting sales and optimizing inventory management.
In summary, the reviewed literature highlights three 
key pillars that guided the application design: (i) the 
superiority of ML approaches over traditional statistical 
methods in volatile demand scenarios, due to their 
ability to capture nonlinear relationships and multiple 
variables [9, 15],(ii) the need to combine algorithmic 
forecasting with human judgment to enhance operational 
decision-making, as supported by studies on managerial 
adjustments in forecasting (Goodwin & Fildes, 2019); and 
(iii) the importance of explainability mechanisms that 
clarify the most influential factors behind the forecast, 
thereby increasing managers’ confidence in the system’s 
recommendations [19].
It is important to emphasize that the majority of the 
cited studies evaluate algorithmic performance (i.e., 
forecasting accuracy of specific ML methods), whereas 
relatively few studies examine the implementation 
process, organizational adoption, or the integration of 
ML-based forecasting into decision making in practice. 
This distinction is critical for positioning the present 
study, whose contribution lies primarily in the domain 
of documenting the feasibility of implementation, 
governance, and socio-technical integration, rather than 
in advancing algorithmic performance per se.
The deployment of automated forecasting systems 
raises 
important 
data 
governance 
questions. 
Organizations must weigh the benefits of automation 
against the risks of over-reliance on algorithmic 
recommendations [19]. Key governance considerations 
include: 
(i) 
Transparency 
and 
explainability: 
Understanding 
how 
forecasts 
are 
generated 
and 
which factors drive predictions; (ii) Monitoring and 
accountability: Establishing responsibility for forecast 
quality and decision outcomes; (iii) Data quality and bias: 
Ensuring input data integrity and detecting potential 
biases in historical patterns; (iv) Human oversight: 
Defining appropriate levels of human intervention 
and override capabilities; and (v) Vendor lock-in: 
Managing dependencies on proprietary platforms and 
ensuring auditability. These data governance aspects 
are particularly critical for SMEs, which may lack 
the technical capacity to audit complex ML systems 
independently. While ML forecasting brings potential 
benefits, a significant adoption gap exists for SMEs 
since traditional ML implementation requires: (i) 
data science expertise for algorithm selection, feature 
engineering, and hyperparameter tuning; (ii) software 
engineering capabilities for production deployment; (iii) 
computational infrastructure; and (iv) ongoing model 
monitoring and maintenance [7].
AutoML platforms aim to democratize ML by 
automating 
algorithm 
selection, 
hyperparameter 
optimization, and model evaluation [11]. Cloud-based 
AutoML services (e.g., AWS SageMaker, Google AutoML, 
and Azure ML) further reduce infrastructure barriers by 
providing managed environments with prebuilt pipelines.
However, research on AutoML adoption in SMEs 
remains limited since most studies focus on algorithmic 
performance rather than on organizational feasibility, 
implementation 
challenges, 
or 
data 
governance 
requirements. The socio-technical aspects of AutoML 
adoption—including user trust, interpretability, and 
integration 
with 
existing 
decision 
processes—are 
particularly underexplored [19].
These findings informed the selection of AutoML 
platforms that integrate diverse algorithms (e.g., 
DeepAR + and random forest) and robust evaluation 
metrics. Although the literature recognizes the potential 
benefits of ML for demand forecasting in complex 
environments, several gaps remain. First, most empirical 
studies focus on large enterprises with dedicated data 
science teams, leaving limited evidence on AutoML

Application development
This section describes the application development 
process, 
covering 
the 
IT 
tools, 
the 
underlying 
architecture, and the performance assessment. The 
application context is Casa Cardão, a hundred-year-old 
Brazilian distributor operating in both wholesale and 
retail channels for construction supplies. It employs 
around 220 staff members and 200 sales representatives. 
It operates two regional distribution centers covering 
12,000 ­m2 of storage and markets a portfolio of over 
10,500 SKUs (Stock Keeping Units), with strong demand 
for 150 items from the ABC curve. In 2023, the company 
generated R$310 million (about US$60 million) in 
revenue and held a 4% market share in Minas Gerais state 
within the cement, mortar, and ceramic tile segments 
[18].
Development tools
Developing and deploying ML models can be complex 
and require substantial computational resources. Cloud 
platforms such as Amazon Web Services (AWS), Google 
Cloud Platform (GCP), and Microsoft Azure offer 
managed services that can simplify this process. In this 
study, two AWS tools were used. Amazon Forecast is a 
fully managed service that uses ML to deliver highly 
accurate time-series forecasts. It automates much of the 
ML process, from data loading to model training and 
deployment, automatically testing various algorithms 
(including ARIMA, ETS, Prophet, DeepAR +, and others) 
and selecting the best one for the data provided [1]. In 
July 2024, AWS closed new customer access to Amazon 
Forecast, directing users to SageMaker Canvas as the 
recommended alternative. Existing Amazon Forecast 
customers can continue using the service, but AWS 
recommends transitioning to SageMaker Canvas for 
enhanced features and cost efficiency. The second tool, 
Amazon SageMaker Canvas, is a no-code visual interface 
that enables business analysts to build ML models 
without writing code. It streamlines data preparation, 
model training, and forecast generation, making ML 
more accessible to non-expert users [2]. These platforms 
facilitated efficient experimentation and implementation 
of predictive models in the Casa Cardão environment.
The project produced a dashboard or visual layer 
that displays structured data collected through an ETL 
(Extract, Transform, and Load) process using AWS 
SageMaker Data Wrangler, covering both external and 
internal data. The dashboard includes a series of graphs 
showing inventory forecasts (Fig. 1). A prompt feature on 
the generative AI-powered dashboard interface provides 
detailed explanations of the methodology and graphs 
used, making the tool more user-friendly for managers.
Additionally, the interface enables managers to run 
simulations as needed, combine data from multiple 
sources, and leverage this information to enhance 
inventory management. The project is delivered in a 
tailored manner, using company-specific data along with 
information from public sources. The conversational 
management dashboard can be continuously improved 
by adding new data sources to develop a more robust and 
effective predictive model for inventory management.
The developed product is a demand prediction 
system that uses predictor results generated by AWS 
SageMaker Canvas, featuring an interactive dashboard 
that provides product demand forecasts across three 
scenarios: optimistic, neutral, and pessimistic. In the 
optimistic scenario, inventory levels are low, and the 
forecast accounts for seasonality and promotions. In the 
pessimistic scenario, inventories are high, and there is no 
forecasted demand for the products. The neutral scenario 
indicates no discernible trend in demand, with stock 
levels remaining balanced. The system’s features include 
time-series analysis, integration of internal and external 
data, customized simulations, and a console that explains 
the methodology and the graphs used.
The project can help the company by lowering costs 
related to excess inventory or product shortages and 
by making inventory management more efficient, 
enabling more confident decision-making. Specifically, 
the goal was to create AI-based inventory management 
software that could predict necessary inventory levels 
with 90% accuracy, and the model achieved a WAPE of 
approximately 0.69% during the evaluation period.
However, this aggregate metric should be interpreted 
with caution, as WAPE is volume-weighted and may be 
disproportionately influenced by high-turnover SKUs, 
potentially masking poorer performance on low-volume

Technology architecture and phases
Application development began in August 2024, and by 
April 2025, the proof of concept (POC) and validation of 
the results were complete. The initial POC was developed 
using Amazon Forecast, but due to the AWS’s transition 
announcement, the production deployment migrated 
to SageMaker Canvas. The system was fully operational 
within the organization by July 2025. Figure 2 illustrates 
the application’s technology pipeline, organized into 
four functional layers in the AWS environment. In the 
ingestion layer, transactional data extracted from Casa 
Cardão’s ERP and CRM systems, along with external 
data such as weather, holidays, and macroeconomic 
indices, are stored in a data lake on Amazon S3. The 
preparation layer uses SageMaker Data Wrangler for 
ETL, ensuring the cleaning, aggregation, and enrichment 
of time-series data. Next, the modeling layer employs 
Amazon SageMaker Canvas for exploratory AutoML 
and the Forecast service (DeepAR +, CNN-QR, Prophet, 
among others) for final training, automatically selecting 
the algorithm with the lowest backtest error [1, 2]. The 
generated forecasts are stored in a versioned S3 bucket, 
and AWS Lambda functions manage daily model 
updates, providing serverless scalability.
At the application layer, the results are integrated 
into an interactive dashboard developed in React on 
the base44 platform. This dashboard displays P35 
(pessimistic), P50 (neutral), P60, and P80 (optimistic) 
scenarios, complemented by a conversational interface 
powered by GPT O3-mini. This modular and fully 
managed workflow facilitates rapid iteration, cost 
efficiency, and seamless integration between algorithmic 
output and human decision making.

The implementation of predictive models using

To demonstrate the model’s performance on a high-
turnover item, the SKU "Amanco 6 m × 100 mm drain

for volume in the Casa Cardão portfolio. The historical

November 2024), totaling 11,074 observations, with 80% 
used for training and 20% for backtesting. The forecast

highlights the synergy between statistical forecasting 
and managerial judgment in selecting the appropriate 
quantile for market conditions.

a separate analysis of intermittent demand items were

Innovation
The innovation contribution of this application lies not in 
algorithmic novelty but in a socio-technical integration 
blueprint for SMEs. Specifically, the innovation involves: 
(i) integrating a data pipeline (ERP/CRM data lake) with 
AutoML platforms and interactive dashboards within 
a coherent data governance workflow; (ii) a human–AI 
decision support design that enables managers without 
technical expertise to select appropriate quantile 
scenarios (P50/P80) and understand forecast implications 
through a conversational interface; and (iii) an empirical 
demonstration of feasibility and practical constraints 
when implementing cloud-based ML in an SME with 
limited IT resources. The use of established cloud 
services (AWS) means that the algorithmic components 
themselves are not novel; rather, the contribution is the 
accessible and reproducible implementation architecture.
The application’s level of innovation is justified 
by making it easier for SMEs to use ML to develop 
predictive models that forecast product demand. In 
demand forecasting, ML can analyze historical data on 
sales, prices, promotions, and other factors to identify 
patterns that help predict future demand.
Using ML for demand forecasting offers several 
advantages over traditional methods. First, it provides 
greater accuracy by considering a wide range of factors 
and identifying patterns that are invisible to the human 
eye. Additionally, machine learning is faster, enabling 
companies to make decisions quickly and respond 
promptly to market changes.
An analysis of predictive model functionality indicates 
that this capability is absent from current enterprise 
resource planning (ERP) systems, which generate reports 
solely from historical data. Likewise, business intelligence 
(BI) systems display only historical data, not data for 
predictive analysis. Although specialized systems that 
cross-reference data and offer predictive models exist, 
their high development costs make this technology 
inaccessible to small and medium-sized enterprises 
(SMEs). Implementing such technologies requires 
experts like data scientists, AI specialists, and cloud 
computing professionals, highly paid professionals that 
many SMEs do not have.

Developing a ML product demand prediction project 
is quite complex, needing close teamwork and diverse 
expertise. It involves collaboration among data scientists, 
software engineers, supply chain specialists, and project 
managers. The involvement of external stakeholders

When implementing the project in other companies, 
some risks should be considered, including poor 
planning, a lack of technical skills, difficulties with 
system integration, and employee resistance to change. 
Integrating with existing systems can lead to problems 
such as incompatibility, failures, and errors. Additionally,

Specific technological risks include data quality,

address these risks, strategies such as proper planning, 
training and capacity building, planned integration,

Discussion of results
Theoretical implications
The results provide preliminary evidence that using 
machine 
learning 
techniques, 
enabled 
by 
cloud 
platforms, can enhance demand forecasting and 
inventory management at a building materials distributor. 
The achieved WAPE of approximately 0.69% represents 
the aggregate error during the evaluation period, though 
this should be interpreted with caution: (i) the metric 
is volume-weighted and may be influenced by high-
turnover items; (ii) a systematic comparison with classical 
baselines (naïve, seasonal naïve) was not conducted; and 
(iii) the error distribution across SKU classes and forecast 
horizons was not analyzed.
Accordingly, the primary value of this study should 
not be assessed through the lens of forecasting accuracy 
alone. The contribution is fundamentally architectural 
and socio-technical since it demonstrates how an 
SME that previously relied exclusively on descriptive 
analytics—historical sales reports generated by its 
ERP system—can transition to a simplified predictive 
analytics 
capability 
using 
cloud-based 
AutoML 
platforms. For Casa Cardão, this leap is analogous 
to moving from a basic point-and-shoot camera to a 
professional camera with guided settings. Analogously, 
the solution does not represent the most sophisticated 
approach in absolute terms, but it provides a relevant, 
accessible, and “palatable” capability gain for the SME 
context. Critically, the implemented architecture enables 
the company to conduct future analyses autonomously, 
without permanent dependence on the research team, 
thereby contributing to organizational learning and 
digital maturity. The novelty of the paper is associated

This approach is expected to work best when: (i)

AutoML enables rapid deployment but limits fine-
grained control over hyperparameters and model 
architectures.

the benefits of ML for capturing complex patterns in 
demand time series [15, 22]. The ability of the algorithms 
used (such as DeepAR +) to automatically incorporate 
features like seasonality, trends, and related metadata (if 
provided) was crucial to the performance achieved.
Using platforms such as Amazon Forecast and 
SageMaker Canvas proved an effective way to overcome 
barriers to ML adoption, especially for SMEs such 
as Casa Cardão. Automating tasks such as algorithm 
selection, hyperparameter tuning, and model evaluation 
(AutoML) accelerated development and enabled strong 
results without requiring a large team of data scientists

Managerial recommendations
From a decision-making perspective, companies must 
balance the trade-offs among processing costs, predictive 
performance, and explainability when selecting cloud 
platforms. Services like Amazon SageMaker Canvas 
offer competitive pay-as-you-go pricing and require 
minimal setup for data pipelines. In contrast, open-
source solutions (e.g., Prophet) offer greater algorithmic 
transparency but depend on an internal data science 
team. For SMEs with limited IT resources, Casa Cardão’s 
experience indicates that outsourcing infrastructure is 
an efficient and replicable way to gradually adopt AI in 
operations.
Based 
on 
our 
implementation 
experience, 
the 
estimated monthly cost of maintaining the AWS 
infrastructure (S3 storage, Lambda functions, SageMaker 
Canvas training and inference) ranges from USD 500 
to USD 1,500, depending on data volume, the number 
of SKUs analyzed, and retraining frequency. This 
budget compares favorably with hiring dedicated data 
science personnel (annual salaries in Brazil for qualified 
professionals exceed USD 60,000), though organizations 
should factor in ongoing cloud costs when assessing 
long-term sustainability beyond initial grant-funded or 
credit-supported phases.

Conclusion
This practice and policy paper describes and analyzes the 
development and deployment of a cloud-based AutoML 
demand forecasting system at Casa Cardão, a medium-
sized Brazilian distributor of building materials. The 
study’s primary contribution is architectural and socio-
technical rather than methodological or algorithmic. 
The originality of the studies lies in providing a detailed 
and reproducible implementation blueprint, showing 
how an SME that previously relied solely on descriptive 
analytics (historical ERP and BI reports) can transition to 
a simplified predictive analytics capability using managed 
cloud platforms (Amazon Forecast and SageMaker 
Canvas). The implemented system achieved a WAPE 
of approximately 0.69% during the evaluation period, 
though this aggregate metric should be interpreted 
cautiously given the limitations discussed below. 
Preliminary qualitative observations suggest potential 
benefits in reducing stockouts, optimizing inventory 
levels, and improving purchasing planning, though 
systematic pre- and post-measurement of quantitative 
business KPIs was not conducted and requires longer 
evaluation periods. The study illustrates how cloud-
based AutoML platforms can lower barriers to entry for 
SMEs that lack specialized Data Science teams, providing 
a concrete and exploratory example of how AI-driven 
demand forecasting can support digital transformation 
in supply chain management. This case study may be 
a motivator for similar SMEs willing to leverage their 
analytic capabilities.
From both theoretical and practical perspectives, 
the study contributes by detailing an end-to-end ML 
implementation in an SME supply chain, including the 
technology architecture, data pipeline, and human–
machine interaction protocols. It reinforces evidence that 
advanced ML models, when combined with managerial 
judgment and appropriate performance monitoring, can 
capture nonlinear and seasonal patterns that traditional 
statistical methods may miss, particularly in volatile 
demand environments such as construction supply 
distribution. The project also highlights the importance 
of 
governance 
mechanisms—such 
as 
continuous 
accuracy monitoring, retraining triggers, and feedback 
loops between forecasts and operational decisions—to 
sustain predictive performance over time.
This study has several limitations that should be 
acknowledged. First, as a single-case practice study, 
the results are context-dependent and should not 
be generalized without validation in other settings. 
Second, only aggregate WAPE is reported; per-SKU 
class breakdowns, intermittent demand analysis, and 
multi-horizon error distributions were not conducted. 
Third, a systematic comparison with naïve and seasonal 
naïve baselines under identical evaluation protocols 
was not performed. Fourth, business impact claims 
(inventory reduction and stockout reduction) are 
presented as expected benefits based on preliminary 
qualitative observations rather than as empirically 
confirmed achievements. Fifth,  reliance on AWS 
AutoML limits methodological transparency, as specific 
hyperparameters were neither manually configured 
nor reported. Sixth, the cold-start problem for new 
products without historical data remains unaddressed. 
Seventh, user adoption and organizational change 
dimensions were not systematically assessed. Eighth, data 
cannot be made publicly available due to confidentiality 
agreements, limiting reproducibility. Finally, the solution 
benefited from temporary AWS credits, and long-term 
financial sustainability at the estimated TCO of USD 
500–1500/month requires validation.
Several 
of 
these 
limitations 
merit 
further 
contextualization. The absence of baseline comparisons 
(third limitation) stems primarily from the “black box” 
nature of the AWS AutoML platform, which does not 
allow users to inject custom algorithms into its evaluation

pipeline or expose how the selected model compares 
against elementary baselines such as naïve persistence. 
This opacity is an inherent feature of proprietary 
AutoML services and represents a trade-off that the 
study consciously accepted in exchange for the feasibility 
of rapid implementation in a resource-constrained SME. 
Similarly, the limited hyperparameter transparency (fifth 
limitation) reflects the design philosophy of AutoML 
platforms, which automate hyperparameter optimization 
as their core value proposition. The evaluation scope 
(second limitation) was constrained to 10 representative 
SKUs from the company’s portfolio of over 10,500 items; 
while these SKUs were selected to represent different 
ABC classification categories, a comprehensive evaluation 
across all demand profiles was beyond the scope of this 
exploratory study. The absence of quantitative business 
impact measurement (fourth limitation) reflects the 
study’s focus on technical feasibility and implementation 
architecture rather than on a full cost–benefit analysis, 
which would require multiple years of post-deployment 
data collection. While significant, these limitations do 
not undermine the study’s core contribution, which is the 
description and analysis of an accessible and reproducible 
implementation blueprint for SME contexts.
Further limitations stem from the technology choices. 
The solution relied primarily on AWS-managed services 
(Amazon Forecast and SageMaker Canvas) and benefited 
from temporary cloud credits, which may not reflect the 
long-term cost structure faced by all SMEs. There is also 
vendor lock-in and opacity associated with proprietary 
algorithms, which can limit transparency, reproducibility, 
and portability to other environments. Additionally, the 
evaluation period for business outcomes was relatively 
short and focused mainly on forecasting metrics and 
qualitative managerial perceptions, rather than on 
a full cost–benefit analysis that included financial 
indicators such as return on investment, working capital 
reduction, or service-level improvements over multiple 
years. Finally, the study did not systematically assess 
organizational change dimensions, such as user adoption, 
learning curves, and the impact of the system on roles, 
routines, and decision-making culture.
These limitations open several opportunities for future 
studies. One important direction is to design, implement, 
and evaluate an open-source reference model that 
replicates the solution’s core functionalities at even lower 
marginal costs and greater transparency. Such a model 
could be built with open-source libraries and frameworks 
and released under a permissive license, enabling 
replication, adaptation, and peer review by researchers, 
practitioners, and policymakers. Comparative studies 
could then benchmark proprietary cloud services against 
fully open-source stacks—considering not only predictive 
accuracy but also total cost of ownership, explainability, 
maintainability, and ease of integration with existing ERP 
and CRM systems.
Future research should also extend the empirical scope 
beyond a single company and sector. Multi-case or cross-
industry studies—including food retail, pharmaceuticals, 
automotive parts, and other sectors with complex, 
intermittent 
demand—could 
test 
the 
proposed 
architecture’s robustness and identify contingencies that 
moderate its effectiveness (e.g., product variety, demand 
volatility, lead times, or data availability). Longitudinal 
designs could assess the long-term financial impact of 
ML-based demand forecasting on inventory turnover, 
stockout rates, service levels, and profitability, ideally 
using quasi-experimental designs or A/B tests comparing 
business units with and without the predictive system.
Another promising line of work integrates demand 
forecasting with optimization models and prescriptive 
analytics. 
Rather 
than 
stopping 
at 
forecasting, 
future applications could combine predictions with 
optimization routines for order quantities, safety stocks, 
and replenishment policies, forming closed-loop decision 
support systems. Research could also delve more deeply 
into socio-technical aspects, including user trust in 
algorithmic recommendations, the design of explainable 
interfaces, 
training 
programs 
for 
managers, 
and 
governance frameworks for AI in SMEs. Finally, policy-
oriented studies could examine how public agencies, 
development banks, and innovation programs might 
support the diffusion of low-cost, open, and responsible 
AI solutions for inventory management among small and 
medium-sized firms.
This study concludes that advanced cloud-based 
ML techniques can substantially improve demand 
forecasting and inventory management for a medium-
sized construction supplies distributor, but it also warns 
that such solutions are not off-the-shelf panaceas. 
Their success depends on data availability and quality, 
careful technology choices, sustained organizational 
engagement, and appropriate governance. Recognizing 
these limitations and exploring the proposed possibilities 
for future work may help researchers, practitioners, 
and policymakers design more robust, transparent, 
and accessible predictive systems—contributing to the 
broader goal of democratizing AI-enabled supply chain 
optimization for SMEs.
